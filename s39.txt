<section class="chapter" id="history">
  <div class="chapter-head"><span class="chapter-num">01</span><h2>How space exploration began</h2></div>
  <p class="lede">The space age started as a race between two nations, and within twelve years it had put a human being on another world.</p>
  <p>The Soviet Union launched the first artificial satellite, Sputnik 1, in October 1957, followed by the first human in orbit, Yuri Gagarin, in April 1961. The United States answered with the Apollo program, which landed Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin on the Moon in July 1969 — still the only time humans have set foot on another world.</p>

<section class="chapter" id="system">
  <div class="chapter-head"><span class="chapter-num">02</span><h2>Our solar system</h2></div>
  <p class="lede">The Sun holds eight planets in orbit, along with countless moons, asteroids, and comets.</p>
  <p>Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are rocky "terrestrial" planets close to the Sun. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune are much larger gas and ice giants farther out, several of them circled by rings made of ice and rock. Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, home to dwarf planets like Pluto.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Mercury and Venus:</strong> The two planets closer to the Sun than Earth; Venus is the hottest planet due to a thick, heat-trapping atmosphere.</li>
    <li><strong>Earth and Mars:</strong> Earth is the only known planet with life; Mars is the most explored planet by robotic rovers.</li>
    <li><strong>Jupiter and Saturn:</strong> The two largest planets, both mostly made of hydrogen and helium gas.</li>
    <li><strong>Uranus and Neptune:</strong> Ice giants with atmospheres of hydrogen, helium, and methane, which gives them a blue tint.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section class="chapter" id="life">
  <div class="chapter-head"><span class="chapter-num">04</span><h2>Living and working in space</h2></div>
  <p class="lede">Space is hostile to human biology, so life on board a station or spacecraft is carefully engineered.</p>
  <p>Astronauts float in microgravity, which causes muscles and bones to weaken over time without daily exercise. Spacecraft recycle air and water, and food is specially packaged to survive without refrigeration. Radiation shielding, temperature control, and communication delays are all challenges that grow harder the farther a mission travels from Earth.</p>
  <div class="callout"><p>Space exploration has produced technology used on Earth every day — including satellite communication, GPS navigation, and improved weather forecasting.</p></div>
</section>

<section class="chapter" id="glossary">
  <div class="chapter-head"><span class="chapter-num">05</span><h2>Glossary of space terms</h2></div>
  <dl class="glossary">
    <div class="gloss-item"><dt>Orbit</dt><dd>The curved path an object follows around a planet or star due to gravity.</dd></div>
    <div class="gloss-item"><dt>Light-year</dt><dd>The distance light travels in one year, used to measure distances between stars.</dd></div>
    <div class="gloss-item"><dt>Gravity assist</dt><dd>Using a planet's gravity to speed up or redirect a spacecraft without using fuel.</dd></div>
    <div class="gloss-item"><dt>Exoplanet</dt><dd>A planet that orbits a star outside our own solar system.</dd></div>
    <div class="gloss-item"><dt>Microgravity</dt><dd>The near-weightless condition experienced in orbit.</dd></div>
    <div class="gloss-item"><dt>Payload</dt><dd>The cargo, instruments, or crew a rocket carries into space.</dd></div>
  </dl>
</section>
